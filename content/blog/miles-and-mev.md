# Miles and mev

Airlines figured out something in the 1980s that crypto is only starting to understand: frequent participation should compound into value.

A loyalty mile is a unit of accumulated engagement. You flew the route, you sat in the seat, you chose this airline over that one. The mile is proof of that choice, and it's redeemable for future value. The entire frequent flyer economy rests on a simple premise: reward the people who show up consistently, and they will keep showing up.

Ethereum has a participation problem that looks surprisingly similar. Every day, users submit transactions that generate mev. Searchers extract it. Builders capture it. Validators collect it. The user — the one who created the opportunity in the first place — gets nothing.

Fast Protocol changes that. Today, at least 90% of the mev your swap generates is returned to you as a direct refund. But what if participation also accumulated? What if the hundredth swap meant something different than the first?

## What are miles in this context?

Think of miles as a tokenized claim on the Fast Protocol treasury.

The protocol generates revenue from every swap. Builders pay a 5% mev fee. Opted-in validators contribute a 5% block fee. This creates a growing protocol treasury — real value, generated by real activity.

Miles represent your share of it. Not speculative tokens minted from nothing. Not governance power over a treasury you'll never vote on. A direct, proportional claim on value that exists because users like you created it.

Every swap you route through Fast Protocol generates measurable mev. Today, most of that goes back to you immediately. Miles would tokenize the remainder — the protocol's cut — and distribute ownership of it to the users who generated it.

## Who earns them?

Users. That's it.

You swap through Fast Protocol, you earn miles. The more you swap, the more you accumulate. Builders and validators play essential roles in the mev supply chain — builders make preconfirmation commitments, validators opt in to propose mev-commit blocks — and they're compensated for those roles through their existing economic incentives. Builders keep healthy margins from mev extraction. Validators see roughly 20x the mev yield compared to non-participants.

But the miles are for the users who create the raw material. Every transaction generates mev. Without users, there is no mev to capture, no bids to commit to, no blocks worth building. Miles recognize that.

Some mev trickles through to builders in the mev-boost bid and to validators in the block reward. That's the existing system working as designed. Miles don't redistribute what builders and validators already earn — they tokenize the protocol treasury that sits on top.

## What would you do with them?

This is where the airline analogy stretches and the crypto-native version gets more interesting.

**Fee tiers.** High-mileage users could access reduced protocol fees. The more value you've contributed over time, the less friction you face on each subsequent transaction. This isn't charity — it's rational. A user who consistently generates mev is more valuable to the network than a one-time swapper.

**Treasury claims.** If miles represent ownership of the protocol treasury, they could be redeemable. Burn miles for a proportional share of accumulated fees. The treasury grows as the protocol grows. Your miles represent your piece of it.

**Priority preconfirmations.** When builder capacity is constrained — during volatile periods, high-gas environments — miles could determine who gets preconfirmed first. Not who pays the most in that instant, but who has the deepest relationship with the protocol.

**Redistribution multipliers.** The current mev redistribution returns value proportionally per swap. Miles could introduce compounding: long-term participants receive a higher share of the redistribution pool, creating a reason to stay rather than hop between protocols.

## The tokenization question

The protocol treasury is real. It has a balance. It grows with usage. The question is how to give users a legible, portable, programmable claim on it.

If miles are standard ERC-20 tokens, they're composable and tradeable — but speculation can overtake utility. People accumulate not to redeem but to sell.

If miles are non-transferable (soulbound), they avoid speculation but lose composability. You can't build secondary markets or use them as collateral.

The design space between these poles is where it gets interesting:

- **Decay.** Miles that depreciate over time, rewarding sustained participation rather than historical accumulation. Use them or lose them. Airlines already do this.
- **Tiered redemption.** Base miles are soulbound claims. Crossing certain thresholds unlocks transferable rewards. The mile stays with you; what it earns can move.
- **Activity-weighted accrual.** Miles earned proportional not just to volume but to the mev your transactions generated. A swap during a high-mev period earns more than a swap during calm conditions, because the protocol treasury grew more from your participation.

## Why this matters now

The mev supply chain on Ethereum is maturing. PBS separated builders and proposers. mev-commit added credible commitments. Fast Protocol brought mev redistribution to end users. Each step makes the system more structured, more measurable, and more fair.

The missing piece is memory. Right now, every transaction is independent. The protocol doesn't know whether you're swapping for the first time or the thousandth. It treats a power user and a tourist identically.

Miles give the protocol memory. They create a legible history of participation that informs how the system treats you going forward. And by tokenizing the protocol treasury, they align long-term user incentives with protocol growth — the more the protocol earns, the more your miles are worth.

Airlines asked this question forty years ago. The answer built one of the most durable loyalty systems in commercial history.

The Fast Protocol treasury is already growing. The accounting infrastructure already exists. The question is whether to surface it as a persistent, compounding ownership layer for the users who built it.
